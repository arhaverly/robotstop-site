Build it or buy it · the honest math

Should you build this yourself? Maybe. Here are the numbers.

A detector costs $2, and your first one is free. Over 10,000 units, it costs about $1.10. Here is where that money goes, what it would cost your team to build the same thing, and the point at which building it yourself is the right call. Some companies are past that point. Most of the ones we talk to are not.

Where your $2 goes

We put the split on the page because the rest of this comparison only works if you can check it.

~$1 Hardware, manufacturing, shipping Parts, board assembly, test, case, box, freight. You would pay this too.
~$1 Software and profit The model, the firmware, the test protocol, updates, support — and our margin.

Over 10,000 units: volume pricing, about $1.10 a unit

Ship more than 10,000 units and we drop the software charge. You pay the ~$1 of hardware, manufacturing and shipping, plus $0.10 a unit for us. That applies to every unit in the order, not just the ones past 10,000.

The hardware dollar does not go away if you build it in house; you still need a microphone, a microcontroller and a board. The software dollar is the only thing building it yourself can save you, and past 10,000 units that is ten cents. So the real question is whether your engineering costs less than what you would save on every unit you ship.

What building it in house costs

A voice stop that works in a demo is a weekend project. One that hears “stop” across a loud room, ignores “don’t stop” and the robot’s own speaker, runs on a microcontroller and fails toward stopping takes a few months. This estimate assumes one engineer who already knows embedded machine learning and doesn’t have to learn it on the job.

Call it $50,000 to $85,000, three to six months, for one language. That doesn’t include keeping it working: firmware fixes, new languages, a new microphone when a part goes end-of-life, or re-testing after each change. It also doesn’t include the safety case, which is the part your safety officer will actually ask about.

Where building it starts to pay

Buying costs $2 a unit after the first, and about $1.10 a unit over 10,000. Building costs your engineering up front plus the same hardware dollar per unit. At volume, each unit you build yourself saves you about ten cents, so you need to ship roughly ten units for every dollar of engineering before building it pays off. At $50,000 of engineering, that is about 500,000 units.

When you should build it yourself

Yes, building it in house can be the right call, and we would rather say so here than waste your time on a call. Build it if:

  • You ship hundreds of thousands of units of one device. With volume pricing you save about ten cents a unit by building, so at $50,000 of engineering it takes about 500,000 units to come out ahead.
  • You already have people who do embedded machine learning and have time on their schedule.
  • The detector has to live on your own main board, sharing a processor you already pay for, so even our hardware dollar goes away.

If two of those are true, building it is probably cheaper, and we will tell you the same thing if you ask.

Who we built this for

Teams that ship fewer than 20,000 units of each device. Under 10,000 units you pay $2 a unit, and at 10,000 that is about $20,000 in total: less than half the low end of the in-house estimate, with nothing to build and no schedule to slip. Past 10,000 it gets cheaper still. At 20,000 units you pay about $22,000, of which about $20,000 is hardware you would have bought anyway, so the software costs you about $2,000.

At that volume, a voice stop is a feature you need, not a product you want to own. Spend the engineering on the robot.

What these numbers assume

  • The $1 / $1 split is approximate and averaged across a production run; it moves a little with volume and freight. Volume pricing is that same ~$1 plus $0.10.
  • Engineering at $3,500 per engineer-week, for someone who has built this kind of system before. Learning on the job costs more.
  • One language, one device, one board design. Each additional language or board adds to the in-house cost, but not to ours.
  • No certification work on either side. Today’s units are uncertified evaluation units; see the terms.
